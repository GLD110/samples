================================================================================
                              ABSTRACT
================================================================================

Commercial banks occupy a central role in modern economies as institutions that
transform short-term, liquid liabilities into longer-term, illiquid assets.
That transformation generates credit risk, liquidity risk, and interest-rate
sensitivity simultaneously. After the global financial crisis of 2007–2009,
macro-prudential policy and stress testing became standard tools for
understanding how adverse macroeconomic scenarios propagate through the banking
sector. Yet students often encounter these ideas as disconnected formulas
rather than as an integrated economic problem.

This thesis develops and documents a browser-based pedagogical artifact—the
Bank Risk Management Dashboard—that makes the joint operation of three stylized
risk channels visible in real time. The model encodes expected credit loss from
portfolio default and recovery assumptions, a deposit-withdrawal liquidity
stress scenario with a simplified bank-run indicator, and a duration-based
approximation of interest-rate loss. Macro-style stress toggles (recession,
bank run, rate shock) adjust inputs before recomputation, illustrating how
policy-relevant scenarios are layered assumptions rather than forecasts. An
optional segment-level "AI Credit Default Engine" uses a transparent linear
scorecard and logistic link to contrast top-down portfolio default rates with
bottom-up borrower heterogeneity—a tension familiar in institutional economics
and credit allocation.

The contribution is primarily pedagogical and interpretive: the dashboard is
not a regulatory reporting system, but a tool for teaching how financial
intermediation, liquidity fragility, and macro shocks interact at a bank. The
thesis situates the artifact in theories of intermediation, deposit insurance
and run dynamics, and post-crisis stress-testing practice, specifies the
economic structure of the simulation, reports illustrative scenarios, and
discusses limitations for classroom and policy literacy.

Keywords: financial intermediation; banking economics; liquidity risk; credit
risk; interest-rate risk; stress testing; macro-prudential policy; financial
education; interactive simulation

================================================================================
                           1. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

Banks are not mere passive holders of deposits; they are economic institutions
that allocate credit, provide payment services, and bear risks arising from
maturity and liquidity mismatch. From an economics perspective, the relevant
questions include: Why do banks exist? How does intermediation affect welfare
and growth? Under what conditions can solvent institutions fail because of
liquidity panics? How do macroeconomic downturns raise default rates and
tighten funding conditions at the same time?

Standard graduate curricula address these topics through models of asymmetric
information (adverse selection and moral hazard in credit markets), models of
bank runs (strategic withdrawal when others withdraw), and empirical accounts
of crises. Regulatory frameworks such as Basel III translate economic concerns
into detailed liquidity and capital rules. For many students, however, the
link between abstract theory and balance-sheet mechanics remains weak.

This thesis accompanies a software artifact designed to narrow that gap. The
Bank Risk Management Dashboard is a single-page web application (React,
TypeScript, Vite) with no backend: users adjust balance-sheet-style inputs and
observe immediate updates to credit loss, liquidity shortfalls, rate-sensitive
losses, and composite indicators. Stress modes operationalize stylized
macro scenarios—recession amplifies default rates, bank run doubles withdrawal
shocks, rate shock adds two percentage points to the assumed rate change—so
students can see how scenario assumptions propagate through multiple channels.

Research questions addressed in this thesis:

  (1) How can a minimal, formula-transparent model represent the joint
      economic pressures on a commercial bank under credit, liquidity, and
      rate stress?

  (2) What does a simplified stress-testing interface teach about the
      difference between base-case balance sheets and adverse macro scenarios?

  (3) How does introducing heterogeneous borrower segments (via a fixed
      scorecard) clarify the economic distinction between aggregate default
      rates and micro-level default probabilities?

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows. Chapter 2 reviews
economic and policy context. Chapter 3 presents the model. Chapter 4 describes
the artifact and methods. Chapter 5 reports scenarios. Chapter 6 discusses
limitations and teaching use. Chapter 7 concludes.

================================================================================
              2. LITERATURE AND ECONOMIC CONTEXT
================================================================================

2.1 Financial intermediation and maturity transformation
----------------------------------------------------------

A large literature explains why intermediaries arise when direct lending is
costly due to information asymmetries. Banks screen and monitor borrowers,
diversify loan portfolios, and offer liquid claims to depositors while holding
illiquid assets (Greenbaum and Thakor; Saunders and Cornett). Maturity
transformation—the use of short-term funding for longer-term lending—is
socially valuable when it supports investment, but it embeds structural
fragility: not every asset can be liquidated quickly without loss.

The dashboard abstracts a bank with total loans L, total deposits D, and cash
reserves C. It does not enforce the accounting identity assets = liabilities +
equity; instead it treats L, D, and C as independent teaching inputs. That
omission is deliberate: the focus is risk metrics under user-chosen scenarios,
not full balance-sheet consistency. Extensions discussed in the conclusion
could add a minimal equity buffer and tie cash to residual assets.

2.2 Liquidity, runs, and depositor coordination
------------------------------------------------

Diamond-Dybvig-style models show that even healthy banks can be destabilized
when depositors coordinate on withdrawals. In practice, deposit insurance,
lender-of-last-resort facilities, and liquidity regulation (e.g., Liquidity
Coverage Ratio under Basel III) mitigate but do not eliminate run risk.

The dashboard's liquidity module computes:

  Liquidity ratio = Cash reserves / Total deposits

  Withdrawals = Total deposits x (Withdrawal shock percent / 100)

  Remaining cash = Cash reserves - Withdrawals

A "bank run" warning appears when remaining cash is negative. This is a
deliberate simplification of inability to meet immediate outflows—it captures
the economic intuition of a liquidity shortfall without modeling queueing,
fire sales, or interbank markets. The bank-run stress mode doubles the
withdrawal shock parameter, analogizing to panic-driven acceleration of
outflows rather than a calibrated historical run.

2.3 Credit cycles and macroeconomic stress
-------------------------------------------

Credit risk rises in recessions as unemployment increases, collateral values
fall, and repayment capacity weakens. Macro stress tests applied by supervisors
after 2008 embed adverse GDP paths, unemployment spikes, and property price
declines into projections of bank losses.

The classic credit module uses a pedagogical expected loss formula:

  EL = Loans x (Default rate / 100) x (1 - Recovery rate / 100)

Interpreting default rate as probability of default (PD) and (1 - recovery) as
loss given default (LGD) links the dashboard to standard credit risk language.
Recession stress multiplies the default rate by 1.8 before calculation—a
teaching knob, not an econometric estimate. Risk bands (low / medium / high)
partition default rates at 2% and 5%, offering an ordinal signal akin to
portfolio quality tiers without a full rating migration model.

2.4 Interest rates, monetary policy, and bank balance sheets
------------------------------------------------------------

When central banks tighten policy, market yields rise and the present value of
fixed-rate assets falls. Banks with rate-sensitive securities and loan books
face market risk even when credit quality is unchanged. A first-order
approximation uses modified duration:

  Change in value ≈ -Duration x Portfolio value x Change in yield

The dashboard reports loss as a positive magnitude:

  Market loss = Portfolio size x Duration x (Rate change / 100)

Rate shock stress adds two percentage points to the rate change input. A
supplementary chart plots loss for parallel shocks of +1%, +2%, +3%, and +4%,
illustrating linear scaling without convexity—adequate for introductory
discussion, insufficient for options-embedded securities.

2.5 Stress testing as economic scenario analysis
------------------------------------------------

Stress testing is not forecasting; it is conditional analysis. Given scenario S,
what happens to metrics M? Supervisors and banks debate scenario plausibility,
correlation across risks, and dynamic balance-sheet responses. The dashboard
implements three independent binary stress flags that adjust inputs before a
single-period recomputation. Correlation between credit, liquidity, and rates
is left to the user: toggling multiple stresses at once mimics a compound
macro shock (e.g., recession plus run plus rate spike) without imposing a
joint distribution.

================================================================================
           3. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK AND MODEL DESIGN
================================================================================

3.1 Economic narrative of the dashboard
---------------------------------------

Imagine a regional bank facing three pressures: borrowers default more often,
depositors withdraw faster, and interest rates rise. Management and regulators
care about expected credit loss, whether cash covers stressed withdrawals, and
mark-to-market or duration-based losses on the rate-sensitive book. A
composite "total risk exposure" sums credit loss, market loss, and any
liquidity shortfall (only negative remaining cash adds a penalty). A heuristic
risk score (0–100) weights default rate, liquidity ratio, and rate change for
dashboard readability—not for binding capital requirements.

3.2 State variables and stress adjustments
------------------------------------------

Base inputs:

  L   = Total loans (credit exposure)
  d   = Default rate (percent)
  rho = Recovery rate (percent)
  D   = Total deposits
  C   = Cash reserves
  w   = Withdrawal shock (percent of deposits)
  r   = Interest rate change (percentage points)
  P   = Loan portfolio size (rate-sensitive book)
  delta = Duration

Stress indicators I_rec, I_run, I_rate (each 0 or 1) yield adjusted inputs:

  d'  = 1.8 * d   if recession; else d
  w'  = 2 * w     if bank run; else w
  r'  = r + 2     if rate shock; else r

Recovery and non-stressed inputs pass through unchanged.

3.3 Credit module as expected loss
---------------------------------

  EL = L * (d'/100) * (1 - rho/100)

A twelve-month default-rate series for charting adds deterministic drift and
small random noise, clipped to [0.2%, 15%]. This series illustrates time
variation; it is not a calibrated ARMA or macro-linked series.

3.4 Liquidity module and run semantics
--------------------------------------

  lambda = C / D
  W = D * (w'/100)
  R = C - W

Bank run flag: true if R < 0. Charts compare cash and withdrawals side by side.

3.5 Market module and rate shocks
---------------------------------

  Loss_market = P * delta * (r'/100)

Chart points use shocks 1%, 2%, 3%, 4% with fixed P and delta.

3.6 Aggregate exposure and composite risk score
-----------------------------------------------

  T = EL + Loss_market + max(0, -R)

  S = clamp(0, 100, (d'/12)*35 + (1 - lambda)*30 + (r'/10)*35)

3.7 Heterogeneous borrowers and the scorecard extension
-------------------------------------------------------

Real credit allocation is heterogeneous: income, leverage, credit history, and
employment stability vary across borrowers. Aggregate portfolio default rates
can mask segment-level distress—a recurring theme in development and household
finance economics.

The optional AI Credit Default Engine assigns each loan segment a probability
of default via a fixed linear score z and logistic transform:

  PD_raw = 1 / (1 + exp(-z))

Features include income category, debt-to-income ratio, credit score, interest
rate, loan amount (for aggregation only), and employment stability. Weights
are fixed for sign intuition, not estimated from data. AI recession stress
multiplies PD by 1.3 (cap at 1); inflation shock scales the interest term;
job market crash halves employment stability before scoring.

Portfolio metrics include average PD, expected default volume, AI expected loss
(using the same recovery as the classic module), and a toy risk-weighted asset
sum (amount x PD x 1.2). Comparing portfolio default rate d'/100 to average PD
illustrates top-down versus bottom-up inconsistency—a practical issue when
macro scenarios and micro scorecards are not reconciled.

================================================================================
              4. SOFTWARE ARTIFACT AND METHODOLOGY
================================================================================

The artifact is implemented in React with TypeScript, bundled by Vite, styled
with Tailwind CSS, and charted with Recharts. Pure functions in src/models/
riskEngine.ts (classic engine) and src/ai/ plus src/simulation/riskEngine.ts
(AI portfolio) separate economic logic from presentation. State lives in React
hooks; useMemo recomputes on input changes.

Methodologically, this thesis is a design-science / pedagogical contribution:
specification of an economic narrative, formal mapping to equations,
implementation, and scenario-based validation. No econometric estimation is
performed. Validation consists of hand-checking numerical examples (Chapter 5)
and transparency of formulas (export to JSON/CSV).

There is no server; the model runs entirely in the browser for classroom demos
without infrastructure. The development server may bind to 0.0.0.0 for LAN access.

================================================================================
        5. ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIOS AND ECONOMIC INTERPRETATION
================================================================================

5.1 Baseline
------------
With all stress modes off, students establish a base case where remaining cash
is non-negative and KPIs reflect entered parameters.

5.2 Recession and credit loss
-----------------------------
Example: L = 100,000,000; d = 4%; rho = 50%.
  EL = 100,000,000 * 0.04 * 0.5 = 2,000,000
Recession mode: d' = 7.2%, raising EL and moving risk from medium to high.
Economic lesson: the same loan book can imply sharply higher expected loss under
a macro scenario without changing ex-post accounting until defaults realize.

5.3 Bank run and liquidity shortfall
------------------------------------
Example: D = 120,000,000; C = 10,000,000; w = 15%.
  Withdrawals = 18,000,000; Remaining cash = -8,000,000 → run warning.
Bank run mode doubles w to 30%, deepening the shortfall. Discuss: deposit
insurance, central bank liquidity, and asset liquidation delays—topics the
toy model omits.

5.4 Rate shock and monetary tightening
-------------------------------------
Example: r = 2%; P = 80,000,000; delta = 4.
  Loss_market = 6,400,000
Rate shock adds 2 points to r', doubling loss in this example. Link to central
bank policy and net interest margin effects (not modeled).

5.5 Compound macro stress
-------------------------
Students may enable recession, bank run, and rate shock together. Total exposure
T rises across channels; the risk score S moves with d', lambda, and r'.
Essay prompt: Are joint stresses realistic without correlation structure? How
would a dynamic model differ?

5.6 Heterogeneity and scorecard checks
--------------------------------------
In AI Risk Mode, raising credit score lowers segment PD; raising DTI or rate
raises PD. Toggling AI recession scales all PDs by 1.3. Comparing classic
portfolio default rate to average PD highlights aggregation bias relevant to
inclusive lending and macro-prudential fairness debates.

================================================================================
     6. POLICY IMPLICATIONS, LIMITATIONS, AND CLASSROOM USE
================================================================================

Policy literacy: The dashboard supports discussion of why Basel liquidity
ratios exist, why stress tests use adverse scenarios, and why bottom-up credit
models may disagree with top-down portfolio rates. It does not compute capital
ratios, resolution planning, or systemic network effects.

Limitations (required for academic honesty):

  1. Single-period static inputs; no multi-year macro path.
  2. No imposed correlation structure beyond user choices.
  3. Credit time series includes illustrative randomness on re-render.
  4. No full Basel capital or LCR/NSFR formulas.
  5. Scalar duration; no prepayment or basis risk.
  6. Stress multipliers (1.8x, 2x, +2%, 1.3x PD, etc.) are arbitrary.
  7. Scorecard weights are not estimated or back-tested.

Classroom use: Verify algebra against KPIs; compare student-proposed stress
calibrations to historical crises; pair with readings on intermediation and
Diamond-Dybvig; use exports for audit assignments. See GUIDE.md and PAPER.md
in the project repository.

================================================================================
                           7. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

This thesis presented an economics-oriented interpretation of the Bank Risk
Management Dashboard—a transparent, browser-based model of credit, liquidity, and
interest-rate stress at a commercial bank. The artifact makes maturity
transformation risks tangible, shows how macro-style scenarios alter metrics,
and optionally illustrates borrower heterogeneity through an auditable
scorecard.

Future work from an economics angle might add balance-sheet identities, a
minimal interbank liquidity market, calibrated macro scenarios from historical
recessions, or agent-based depositor behavior. For policy education, the
current design prioritizes clarity over realism—a trade-off appropriate for
master's-level foundations before students encounter enterprise risk systems.
