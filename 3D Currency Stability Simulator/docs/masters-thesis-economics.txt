ABSTRACT
================================================================================

Emerging and middle-income economies frequently face coupled pressures on
exchange rates, inflation, foreign reserves, and domestic currency use. Yet
undergraduate and professional audiences often struggle to internalize how
policy levers interact over time because conventional textbooks present static
diagrams and isolated models. This thesis develops and evaluates a client-side
"3D Currency Stability Simulator": a monthly discrete-time macro sandbox whose
state variables (inflation, exchange rate, dollarization, reserves, trust,
and a composite stability score) evolve deterministically from six normalized
policy controls. The contribution is not econometric forecasting but pedagogical
and communicative: linking reduced-form macro relationships to an immersive
three-dimensional city metaphor in which building scale, particle flows, import
networks, and crisis visuals encode macro stress.

The economic model is intentionally illustrative. Each simulated month updates
FX pressure from import dependence, inflation, USD preference, intervention,
and reserve weakness; panic amplifies depreciation and inflation feedback;
dollarization rises with inflation and preference and falls with trust;
reserves deplete under sustained pressure unless intervention rebuilds them.
Five scenario presets (stable economy, emerging market stress, import crisis,
hyperinflation spiral, currency collapse) anchor comparative experiments. The
implementation runs entirely in the browser without external data feeds,
supporting classroom deployment and reproducible demonstrations.

The thesis argues that spatial, real-time visualization can complement
standard open-economy macro instruction by making path dependence and policy
trade-offs salient—particularly the tension between FX intervention and reserve
buffers, and between money-financed deficits and inflation/dollarization
spirals. Limitations, extensions toward empirical calibration, and ethical
uses in policy communication are discussed.

Keywords: open-economy macroeconomics, currency crisis, dollarization, FX
intervention, inflation dynamics, economic education, interactive simulation

1. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

1.1 Motivation

Currency instability in open economies rarely manifests as a single shock.
Instead, governments, firms, and households confront interacting forces:
imported inflation when the exchange rate weakens, declining confidence in
local money, pressure to invoice and save in foreign currency, and finite FX
reserves that constrain official intervention. Standard macro curricula cover
these themes through the Mundell–Fleming framework, balance-of-payments
crises, and models of dollarization and hysteresis, yet students often treat
each topic in isolation.

Interactive simulation can restore the temporal structure of macro adjustment.
When learners adjust "money printing," "import dependency," or "trust in local
currency" and observe months unfold, they encounter complementarities and
feedback that static figures understate. This project embeds such a simulation
in a three-dimensional environment so that macro variables have immediate visual
correlates—city height for GDP strength, blue versus green particles for USD
versus local currency use, thick import lines for trade exposure, fog and camera
shake for systemic stress.

1.2 Research Questions

RQ1: Can a reduced-form monthly macro sandbox capture qualitatively plausible
     currency-stability dynamics across a range of emerging-market stress
     scenarios?

RQ2: Does coupling the sandbox to spatial visualization improve comprehension
     of policy trade-offs compared with numeric dashboards alone?

RQ3: What are the boundaries of illustrative simulation for economics
     instruction and public communication?

1.3 Contributions

- A documented, open, client-side macro sandbox with transparent update rules.
- A mapping from macro state to visual encodings grounded in economic metaphor.
- Scenario presets that span stable equilibria through near-total dollarization.
- A critical discussion of illustrative models versus structural estimation.

1.4 Thesis Structure

Chapter 2 situates the work in currency-crisis and dollarization literature.
Chapter 3 presents the economic model. Chapter 4 explains visualization as
narrative. Chapter 5 describes software architecture. Chapter 6 discusses
scenarios. Chapter 7 addresses evaluation and limits. Chapter 8 concludes.

2. LITERATURE AND CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK
================================================================================

2.1 Open-Economy Macro and Crises

The first-generation balance-of-payments crisis literature (Krugman, 1979;
Flood and Garber, 1984) emphasizes inconsistent fiscal/monetary policy and
exhaustible reserves. Second-generation models (Obstfeld, 1994) add multiple
equilibria and self-fulfilling expectations. While the simulator does not
estimate structural parameters, it incorporates reserve depletion, FX pressure,
and depreciation—themes consistent with crisis narratives in emerging markets.

2.2 Inflation, Pass-Through, and Import Dependence

High import shares amplify exchange-rate pass-through to domestic prices. The
simulator's "imported inflation" term scales with import dependency and the
gap between current and anchor exchange rates, modulated by intervention.
This aligns with reduced-form evidence that pass-through rises with openness
and falls with credible stabilization (Burstein and Gopinath, 2014).

2.3 Dollarization and Currency Substitution

Currency substitution—using foreign money for transactions and store of value—
can persist after stabilization (Calvo and Vegh, 1992; Guidotti and Rodriguez,
1992). The model's USD usage evolves with inflation, USD preference, panic, and
trust in the local currency, capturing a stylized substitution dynamic without
household portfolio optimization.

2.4 Trust, Institutions, and Macro Stability

Trust in local currency is treated as a slow-moving social/state variable eroded
by inflation, FX weakness, and panic, and supported by policy credibility
proxied through the "trust in local" control. This echoes institutional
accounts of monetary legitimacy without modeling explicit game-theoretic
signaling.

2.5 Visualization in Economics Education

Research on simulation-based learning suggests active experimentation improves
retention of dynamic systems (Witt, 2003; Goffe and Sosin, 2005). Immersive
visualization may further leverage spatial cognition, though evidence specific
to 3D macro tools remains limited—motivating the evaluative stance in Chapter 7.

3. MODEL SPECIFICATION AND ECONOMIC INTERPRETATION
================================================================================

3.1 Time Structure and State Vector

Time is discrete with unit period "one month." State at month t:

  - inflation (annualized decimal)
  - exchangeRate (units per USD anchor; baseline 1)
  - usdUsage, localUsage (shares in [0,1])
  - fxReserves (normalized buffer)
  - trust (confidence in local currency)
  - fxPressure (composite stress index)
  - stabilityScore (0–100 composite welfare/policy indicator)
  - gdpIndex (visual output; stylized activity index)

Controls (all normalized 0–1) represent policy and structural conditions:

  - importDependency
  - moneyPrinting (fiscal/monetary financing pressure)
  - fxIntervention (official market support)
  - trustInLocal (credibility/communication/policy backing)
  - usdPreference (structural bias toward USD)
  - panicSensitivity (behavioral amplification of stress)

3.2 FX Pressure

FX pressure aggregates openness to imports, inflation, USD preference,
intervention (negative), and reserve weakness. It functions as a sufficient
statistic for near-term depreciation risk in the reduced form.

3.3 Panic and Expectations

Panic rises with FX pressure and inflation, scaled by panic sensitivity. It
feeds depreciation and inflation, modeling procyclical expectation shocks
without an explicit survey-expectations block.

3.4 Exchange Rate Dynamics

Depreciation is proportional to excess pressure above a threshold plus panic.
A floor prevents numerical collapse; economically, this is a numerical safeguard
rather than a long-run purchasing-power-parity anchor.

3.5 Inflation Dynamics

Inflation updates via: (i) money printing with state-dependent acceleration;
(ii) imported inflation; (iii) panic feedback; (iv) partial offset from
intervention (interpreted as coordinated stabilization credibility or subsidized
energy imports—kept deliberately reduced-form).

3.6 Dollarization

USD share increases with inflation, preference, and panic; decreases with
trust in local. Complement local usage = 1 - usdUsage.

3.7 Reserves and Intervention

Reserves fall when pressure exceeds a buffer threshold; intervention adds
reserves but effectiveness is damped when reserves are already low (implemented
via pressure and depreciation interactions). Large depreciations trigger extra
reserve loss scaled by import dependency—capturing import-bill and roll-over
stress.

3.8 Stability and GDP Index

Stability score penalizes inflation, FX pressure, dollarization, low trust, and
rewards reserves. GDP index co-moves for visualization; it is not national
accounts GDP.

3.9 Equilibrium Concepts

The system is generally non-stationary under fixed controls: trajectories may
drift toward high-inflation, high-dollarization regions or remain in moderate
bands depending on parameters. Multiple "qualitative equilibria" appear as
attractor-like regions in scenario presets rather than formal bifurcation
analysis.

4. VISUALIZATION DESIGN AS ECONOMIC NARRATIVE
================================================================================

4.1 City as Aggregate Activity

Buildings represent stylized sectors; height tracks gdpIndex, color/glow
reflect stability and inflation, lean reflects FX pressure, flicker reflects
inflation volatility. This encodes Okun-type intuition: macro instability
correlates with activity loss.

4.2 Particles as Monetary Circulation

Green particles: local currency flows. Blue particles: USD flows. Mix shifts
with dollarization; chaos rises with inflation and panic—making substitution
visible.

4.3 Import Network

Peripheral nodes and lines into the center visualize trade dependence; line
thickness scales with import dependency. Under stress, color shifts signal
external vulnerability.

4.4 Crisis Layer

Fog, camera shake, and heat distortion communicate systemic risk without
replacing quantitative HUD metrics (month, inflation, FX, reserves, trust,
stability).

4.5 Pedagogical Principle

Every visual channel maps to a named economic state variable documented in the
user guide, preserving analytical rigor while supporting intuitive exploration.

================================================================================
5. IMPLEMENTATION ARCHITECTURE
================================================================================

5.1 Client-Only Execution

The application uses a Vite-hosted React front end with Three.js/React Three
Fiber for rendering. Economic updates occur in tickSimulation (simulation.ts)
invoked monthly by a clock component or manual step. Zustand stores state and
history for sparkline analytics.

5.2 Determinism and Reproducibility

Given initial state and controls, paths are deterministic—useful for classroom
demonstrations and scripted scenarios.

5.3 Extensibility

Researchers may recalibrate coefficients, add fiscal blocks, or export history
for offline plotting. No proprietary data dependencies impede replication.

================================================================================
6. SCENARIO ANALYSIS AND PEDAGOGICAL USE CASES
================================================================================

6.1 Preset Scenarios

(1) Stable Economy: low stress, high trust and reserves.
(2) Emerging Market Stress: moderate inflation and FX pressure.
(3) Import Crisis: high pass-through, weakening buffers.
(4) Hyperinflation Spiral: extreme printing and panic.
(5) Currency Collapse: near-complete dollarization and reserve exhaustion.

6.2 Classroom Exercises

- Hold intervention constant; raise import dependency and document reserve paths.
- Compare money printing shocks under high versus low trustInLocal.
- Contrast panic sensitivity in otherwise identical economies (multiple-equilibria
  intuition).
- Reset and replicate preset paths for discussion of early warning indicators.

6.3 Policy Communication

The tool can illustrate trade-offs in public forums when accompanied by explicit
disclaimers that coefficients are illustrative, not estimated from national data.

================================================================================
7. EVALUATION, LIMITATIONS, AND EXTENSIONS
================================================================================

7.1 Limitations

- Not a forecasting model; no econometric identification.
- Monthly steps abstract intra-month volatility and policy announcement effects.
- No explicit external debt, default, or banking sector.
- Parameters are tuned for pedagogical clarity, not fit to a specific country.
- 3D visuals may distract some users; accessibility requires text equivalents
  (HUD and analytics panel partially address this).

7.2 Proposed Empirical Extensions

- Calibrate pass-through and intervention coefficients to SVAR/event studies.
- Feed initial states from World Bank/IMF series (reserves months of imports).
- Add welfare metrics (consumer surplus proxies, Gini-neutral assumptions).

7.3 Evaluation Design (Future Work)

Pre/post tests on identifying correct policy responses to reserve depletion;
A/B comparison of 3D versus tabular UI; expert review by development economists.

================================================================================
8. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

This thesis presents an economics-oriented account of a 3D currency stability
simulator that makes open-economy feedback loops tangible. By documenting the
reduced-form model, visual encodings, and scenario library, it supports
reproducible teaching and critical engagement with macro policy under stress.
The central claim is modest but actionable: temporal, interactive, and spatially
encoded macro models can improve policy literacy when their illustrative nature
is transparent. Future work should pair the interface with empirical calibration
and rigorous learning assessment.

APPENDIX B: MONTHLY UPDATE EQUATIONS (REDUC FORM)
================================================================================

Let controls and state be as implemented in tickSimulation:

FX pressure:
  pressureRaw = 0.38*importDependency + 0.22*min(inflation,3) + 0.24*usdPreference
                  - 0.5*fxIntervention + 0.12*(1 - fxReserves)
  fxPressure = clamp01(pressureRaw)

Panic:
  panicBase = 0.55*fxPressure + min(inflation/2, 0.45)*(0.4 + 0.4*panicSensitivity)
  panic = clamp01(panicBase * (0.5 + 0.5*panicSensitivity))

Exchange rate (depreciation if pressure high):
  erStress = (fxPressure - 0.25)*0.055 + panic*0.02
  exchangeRate <- max(0.15, exchangeRate * (1 + erStress))

Imported inflation:
  importedInflation = importDependency * max(0, exchangeRate - 1) * 0.08
                      * (1 - 0.4*fxIntervention)

Inflation:
  inflation += moneyPrinting*0.045*(1 + 0.15*inflation)
  inflation += importedInflation + panic*panicSensitivity*0.025
  inflation -= fxIntervention*0.012
  inflation <- max(0.002, inflation)

USD usage:
  usdUsage += inflation*0.04*usdPreference + (1 - trustInLocal)*0.018 + panic*0.035
  usdUsage -= trustInLocal*0.008
  usdUsage <- clamp(usdUsage, 0.02, 0.98)

Reserves:
  fxReserves -= max(0, fxPressure - 0.28)*0.022
  fxReserves += fxIntervention*0.028
  if depreciation > 8% in one month: fxReserves -= 0.015*importDependency

Trust:
  trust <- 0.88*trust + 0.12*trustInLocal - 0.06*inflation
           - max(0, exchangeRate - 1.2)*0.025 - 0.04*panic

Stability score and GDP index: composite functions of inflation, pressure,
dollarization, trust, and reserves (see source for coefficients).
