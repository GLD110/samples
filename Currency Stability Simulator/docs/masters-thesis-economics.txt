                              ABSTRACT
================================================================================

Emerging and developing economies frequently face intertwined pressures on
exchange rates, inflation, and currency usage. Weak external balances, import
dependence, loss of confidence in local money, and aggressive fiscal
monetization can reinforce one another in destabilizing feedback loops. This
thesis develops and analyzes a stylized discrete-time macroeconomic simulation
—the Currency Stability Simulator—designed to make those causal chains
visible for teaching and policy intuition.

The model represents a small open economy in monthly steps. Structural
parameters capture import dependency, productive capacity (GDP strength),
foreign-exchange reserve buffers, monetary policy (money printing and interest
rates), official FX intervention, public trust in the local currency, USD
preference, and panic sensitivity. State variables track inflation, the
exchange rate (local units per US dollar), dollarization (USD vs. local usage
shares), an import cost index, and a composite stability score. External shocks
(oil price spikes, war/import disruption, IMF bailouts) enter as temporary
modifiers.

Each simulation tick applies a fixed causal sequence: FX pressure is computed
from structural pulls and policy buffers; the exchange rate depreciates when
pressure exceeds interest-rate and reserve support; depreciation passes through
to inflation via import costs; inflation feeds back into FX pressure and
dollarization; and stability is re-scored. The engine distinguishes four system
statuses—Stable, Weak, Crisis, and Collapse—using threshold rules on inflation,
stability, FX pressure, and the exchange-rate level.

Scenario experiments with five presets (Stable Economy, Emerging Market Stress,
Import Crisis, Hyperinflation Spiral, Peg Collapse) illustrate how identical
policy instruments produce divergent paths depending on initial structure and
expectations. The thesis argues that interactive simulation complements
analytical open-economy models by emphasizing path dependence, non-linear
amplification through panic, and the limits of intervention without reserves.

The model is explicitly pedagogical: coefficients are calibrated for clarity
of feedback, not econometric fit to any single country. Nevertheless, the
framework maps cleanly onto standard macroeconomic concepts—pass-through,
monetary accommodation, currency substitution, and balance-of-payments stress—
and provides a reproducible laboratory for comparative statics and shock
analysis.

Keywords: open-economy macroeconomics, exchange-rate pass-through, inflation
dynamics, dollarization, currency crisis, computational simulation, policy
credibility

================================================================================
                          1. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

1.1 Motivation

Currency instability is rarely a single-equation problem. Depreciation raises
import prices; higher prices erode real wages and confidence; households and
firms shift toward foreign currency; dollarization increases effective demand
for dollars and weakens monetary transmission in local currency; authorities
face a trilemma between defending the exchange rate, controlling inflation, and
preserving growth. Historical episodes—from Latin American hyperinflations to
recent emerging-market stress—show that these channels can interact faster
than linear spreadsheet models suggest.

Traditional undergraduate and graduate macroeconomics often treats exchange
rates, inflation, and money demand in separate modules. Students may master
each block in isolation yet struggle to predict how a reserve drain, an oil
shock, and a credibility loss compound over twelve to twenty-four months. This
thesis addresses that gap with a transparent, open-source simulation whose
equations are small enough to read in one sitting but rich enough to generate
crisis dynamics.

1.2 Research Questions

  RQ1: Which structural features most strongly determine whether an economy
       remains Stable or transitions to Crisis or Collapse under sustained
       stress?

  RQ2: How do trust, panic sensitivity, and USD preference alter the speed and
       severity of inflation–FX feedback loops?

  RQ3: Under what conditions can higher interest rates and FX intervention
       stabilize the exchange rate without triggering an inflation spiral or
       accelerating dollarization?

  RQ4: How do temporary external shocks (commodity prices, trade disruption,
       official financing) shift the trajectory relative to baseline parameters?

1.3 Contribution

The contribution is threefold:

  (1) A documented discrete-time model linking FX pressure, pass-through
      inflation, currency substitution, and a stability index in one engine.

  (2) A set of reproducible scenario presets and shock modules that encode
      common emerging-market narratives.

  (3) An pedagogical interpretation connecting simulation coefficients to
      established open-economy theory (Mundell–Fleming intuition, pass-through
      literature, currency-substitution models).

1.4 Scope and Disclaimer

The Currency Stability Simulator is not a forecasting tool. It does not
estimate parameters from national accounts or market data. All results are
illustrative. The thesis focuses on comparative dynamics and teaching value.

================================================================================
                       2. LITERATURE REVIEW
================================================================================

2.1 Exchange-Rate Pass-Through and Import Prices

A large empirical literature documents incomplete and time-varying pass-through
from exchange rates to consumer prices (Goldberg and Knetter, 1997; Burstein
and Gopinath, 2014). Import-dependent economies exhibit stronger near-term
pass-through, especially when domestic competition is weak or mark-ups adjust
slowly. The simulation encodes this via import dependency scaling the
inflation response to monthly depreciation, moderated by trust in local
currency (a reduced-form proxy for pricing-to-market and indexation).

2.2 Currency Substitution and Dollarization

Currency substitution models (Calvo and Vegh, 1992; Guidotti and Rodriguez,
1992) emphasize that when domestic money fails as a store of value, agents
shift to foreign currency, weakening seigniorage and monetary control. The
simulator's USD usage variable aggregates transaction and portfolio
substitution into a single share, driven by inflation, trust gaps, USD
preference, FX pressure, and partially offset by interest rates.

2.3 Balance-of-Payments Crises and Reserve Exhaustion

First- and second-generation currency crisis models (Krugman, 1979; Obstfeld,
1994) highlight inconsistent policy mixes and self-fulfilling attacks. The
peg-collapse preset mirrors a country defending an overvalued rate with
intervention while reserves erode—a stylized second-generation narrative. FX
intervention effectiveness in the model is scaled by effective reserve strength,
capturing the idea that promises to defend a peg without ammunition lack
credibility.

2.4 Inflation Spirals and Fiscal Dominance

Sargent and Wallace (1981) and modern fiscal theory of the price level remind
us that monetization of deficits can destabilize inflation expectations. The
money-printing parameter represents excess liquidity creation; combined with
panic-driven spiral terms tied to existing inflation, the model can generate
accelerating paths resembling fiscal dominance without explicit government
budget accounting.

2.5 Agent-Based and System-Dynamics Approaches

Beyond DSGE estimation, system-dynamics and agent-based models (Epstein, 2006;
Farmer and Foley, 2009) prioritize feedback visibility. This thesis aligns
with that tradition: low dimensionality, explicit causal ordering, and UI-
driven experimentation over maximum realism.

================================================================================
                      3. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
================================================================================

3.1 Conceptual Causal Graph

The engine implements the following monthly causal chain:

  Structural parameters + shocks
           |
           v
      FX pressure (0–100 index)
           |
           v
   Exchange rate (depreciation)
           |
           +----> Import cost index
           |
           v
      Inflation (annualized display)
           |
           +----> FX pressure (feedback)
           |
           v
   USD vs. local usage (dollarization)
           |
           v
      Stability score + system status

3.2 FX Pressure as a Reduced-Form Balance-of-Payments Stress Index

FX pressure aggregates "pull" forces—import dependency, current inflation,
USD preference—amplified by panic sensitivity. It subtracts "buffer" forces:
FX intervention (interacting with reserves), GDP strength, reserve levels, and
trust. The result is clamped to [0, 100]. Higher pressure maps to stronger
monthly depreciation unless offset by interest-rate support and reserve-backed
stabilization.

Economically, pressure stands in for net external demand for foreign currency
relative to policy capacity to supply or absorb it.

3.3 Inflation Block

Monthly inflation accumulates from:

  - Monetary push (money printing rate)
  - Exchange-rate pass-through (imports × depreciation)
  - Interest-rate disinflation
  - Panic spiral (function of existing inflation and panic sensitivity)
  - Shock bonuses (oil, war, IMF)

Displayed inflation blends toward an annualized equivalent derived from monthly
flow, smoothing short spikes while preserving trend feedback into FX pressure.

3.4 Dollarization Block

USD usage rises with inflation stress, low trust, high USD preference, and FX
pressure; it falls slightly with higher local interest rates and trust. Local
usage is the residual to 100%. This is a reduced-form law of motion, not a
micro-founded portfolio choice model.

3.5 Stability Score and Regime Classification

Stability penalizes high inflation, high USD share, and high FX pressure; it
rewards GDP strength, trust, and reserves. Threshold rules classify outcomes:

  Stable   — moderate inflation, adequate stability, moderate pressure
  Weak     — elevated inflation and/or pressure, or slipping stability
  Crisis   — low stability, high inflation, and/or very high pressure
  Collapse — extreme inflation, very low stability, or exchange rate > 8× initial

================================================================================
                       4. MODEL SPECIFICATION
================================================================================

4.1 State Variables (updated each tick)

  month              — elapsed simulation months
  inflationPct       — approximate annualized inflation (%)
  exchangeRate       — local currency per 1 USD (initial: 100)
  usdUsage           — share of activity in USD (%)
  localUsage         — 100 − usdUsage
  importCostIndex    — normalized import burden index (initial: 100)
  stabilityScore     — composite 0–100 (initial: 78)

4.2 Parameters (user-controlled sliders, 0–100 unless noted)

Economy structure:
  importDependency   — reliance on imported goods/inputs
  gdpStrength        — resilience / shock absorption
  fxReserveStrength  — external buffer capacity

Monetary policy:
  moneyPrintingRate  — liquidity / monetization looseness
  interestRate       — policy tightness
  fxInterventionStrength — official lean against depreciation

Public behavior:
  trustInLocalCurrency — willingness to hold local money
  usdPreference      — taste for dollar assets and invoicing
  panicSensitivity   — amplification of stress responses

4.3 Shock Modifiers (temporary, stackable)

  Oil price spike (6 months):
    +14 effective import dependency; +0.12 monthly inflation bonus

  War / import disruption (8 months):
    +22 import dependency; +0.20 monthly inflation; −6 reserves

  IMF bailout (10 months):
    +28 reserves; −0.10 monthly inflation

4.4 Key Equations (conceptual summary)

Effective import dependency and reserves apply shock bonuses then clamp to
[0, 100].

FX pressure = clamp(pulls × panicAmplify − buffers, 0, 100)

Net monthly depreciation = max(0, pressureDepreciation − rateSupport − reserveSupport)

New exchange rate = old rate × (1 + netMonthlyDepreciation)

Import cost index = (new rate / initial rate) × effective import dependency

Stability = clamp(100 − penalties + rewards, 0, 100)

Full coefficient values are implemented in src/engine/simulation.ts.

================================================================================
                    5. SIMULATION METHODOLOGY
================================================================================

5.1 Discrete-Time Stepping

Time advances in uniform monthly ticks. Users may run continuously (1×, 2×, 5×
speed), step one month manually, or reset while retaining parameters. History
retains the last 100 months for charting.

5.2 Initial Conditions

Default initial state: month 0, inflation 6%, exchange rate 100, USD usage 28%,
import cost index 100, stability 78. Presets replace both parameters and initial
state.

5.3 Identification Strategy (Illustrative)

Because the model is not econometrically estimated, "results" are comparative
statics across presets and shock combinations. Experiments hold all parameters
fixed except the dimension under study, then run forward 24–60 months.

5.4 Implementation

The engine is written in TypeScript, executed client-side in a web application.
Persistence uses browser localStorage. This ensures reproducibility of scenarios
without a server-side calibration pipeline.

================================================================================
                  6. SCENARIO ANALYSIS AND RESULTS
================================================================================

6.1 Stable Economy Preset

Low import dependency (28), strong GDP and reserves, moderate monetization,
high trust, low panic. Typical outcome: exchange rate drifts modestly;
inflation remains single-digit to low double-digit; USD share stable; status
Stable. Serves as control case.

6.2 Emerging Market Stress

Higher imports, thinner reserves, more printing, elevated USD preference and
panic. FX pressure rises within 6–12 months; inflation accelerates; USD usage
climbs; status transitions Weak → Crisis unless policy sliders tightened
mid-run.

6.3 Import Crisis

Very high import dependency (78), weak reserves and trust. Import cost index
and FX pressure co-move sharply with even moderate depreciation. Demonstrates
the import–FX loop central to RQ1: structure dominates when buffers are thin.

6.4 Hyperinflation Spiral

Extreme money printing (88), low trust and reserves, high panic and USD
preference. Inflation spiral term dominates mid-run; dollarization exceeds 50%;
stability collapses; Collapse status often within 18–30 months in default
runs.

6.5 Peg Collapse

High intervention (72) with low reserves (32) and elevated panic. Intervention
relief is limited by reserve scaling; latent pressure accumulates; sudden
depreciation phases possible when pressure persistently exceeds support—
mirroring delayed peg failures.

6.6 Shock Experiments

From Stable Economy baseline:

  - Oil spike alone: temporary inflation and import index rise; recovery if
    shock expires before panic amplifies feedback.

  - War disruption: stronger and longer stress; reserve penalty can push
    intervention effectiveness below perceived commitment.

  - IMF bailout: partial stabilization; inflation bonus reduction and reserve
    boost buy time but do not replace structural reform (low import dependency
    and trust still required for long-run stability).

6.7 Policy Tradeoffs (RQ3)

Raising interest rates reduces net depreciation and damps inflation but may be
insufficient if reserves are depleted and import dependency is high. FX
intervention without reserves produces minimal pressure relief in the formula
(intervention × reserves interaction). Trust acts as a cross-cutting buffer:
high trust reduces pass-through, slows dollarization, and lowers FX pressure.

================================================================================
                       7. POLICY IMPLICATIONS
================================================================================

7.1 Structural vs. Cyclical Policy

The simulations reinforce that cyclical tightening cannot fully offset weak
structure. High import dependency and low trust create conditions where standard
rate hikes slow but do not reverse spirals.

7.2 Credibility and Expectations

Trust and panic parameters function as expectation shifters. Policies that
restore credibility (fiscal consolidation, independent central bank, transparent
FX regime) align with lower panic sensitivity in practice—modeled here as
exogenous slider improvements.

7.3 Shock Preparedness

Building reserve buffers and diversifying import sources reduce vulnerability to
oil and supply shocks. The IMF shock module illustrates short-term liquidity
relief without guaranteed exit from Weak status if underlying parameters remain
stressed.

7.4 Dollarization as Policy Constraint

Rising USD usage erodes stability score directly and reflects shrinking monetary
sovereignty. Re-dollarization requires sustained inflation control and trust
recovery, not only a one-off devaluation.

================================================================================
                   8. LIMITATIONS AND EXTENSIONS
================================================================================

8.1 Limitations

  - No explicit labor market, output gap, or welfare metrics
  - No government budget constraint or debt dynamics
  - Single foreign currency (USD); no euro or regional currencies
  - Reduced-form behavioral parameters rather than estimated elasticities
  - No spatial heterogeneity or distributional effects
  - Depreciation floor at zero net monthly change (no sustained appreciation path)

8.2 Possible Extensions

  - Endogenize trust as a function of past inflation and policy surprises
  - Add wage–price indexation channel
  - Introduce capital controls as additional policy slider
  - Export simulation trajectories for econometric comparison with country panels
  - Stochastic shocks for Monte Carlo crisis probability

================================================================================
                          9. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

This thesis presented an economics-oriented analysis of the Currency Stability
Simulator, a discrete-time model of FX pressure, pass-through inflation,
dollarization, and composite stability. By making feedback loops explicit and
interactive, the tool helps students and policymakers develop intuition for
path-dependent crises that static models underemphasize.

Scenario analysis across five presets and three shock types supports four
qualitative conclusions:

  1. External structure (imports, reserves) sets the baseline vulnerability.
  2. Expectations and panic amplify otherwise manageable shocks.
  3. Intervention credibility requires real buffers, not declared intent alone.
  4. Dollarization is both symptom and accelerator of instability.

Future work should link selected coefficients to meta-analytic priors from
pass-through and crisis literature, while preserving the simulator's
transparency. Even as a stylized teaching instrument, the model clarifies why
currency stability is a system property—not a single policy lever.
