ABSTRACT
================================================================================

From a finance perspective, hyperinflation is an extreme regime in which the domestic
currency fails as a store of value, real money balances collapse, and asset-allocation
decisions dominate macro outcomes. This thesis analyses the Hyperinflation Control
Simulator—a browser-based policy dashboard—as a stylised laboratory for currency
risk, purchasing-power loss, and credibility-driven velocity dynamics.

The model tracks a currency purchasing-power index, money supply growth, and a
composite “currency health” metric derived from confidence and inflation. Policy
instruments (money printing, fiscal spending, interest rates, taxes) map to paths
that resemble distressed sovereign finance: monetised deficits, negative real rates,
and feedback between inflation and confidence. Three presets (stable, crisis, spiral)
demonstrate how financial fragility escalates when monetisation outpaces real growth
and institutional trust erodes.

The thesis concludes that the simulator is a valuable teaching and risk-intuition
tool for finance students studying emerging-market crises, real-asset hedging, and
central-bank credibility, provided its reduced-form structure and lack of market
microstructure are clearly acknowledged.

Keywords: currency risk, purchasing power, velocity, sovereign finance, inflation
hedging, policy credibility, simulation, financial stability.

1. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

1.1 Problem Statement

Finance practitioners and researchers encounter hyperinflation primarily through
currency collapse, capital flight, dollarisation, and the repricing of nominal claims.
Unlike a gradual rise in CPI, hyperinflation destroys the unit of account within
months or weeks, forcing indexation, foreign-currency contracts, and real-asset
substitution.

Understanding how policy choices accelerate or contain this process is essential for
sovereign risk analysis, emerging-market debt, and inflation-linked instrument design.

1.2 Objectives

This thesis:

  (1) Interprets the Hyperinflation Control Simulator in financial terms—currency
      depreciation of purchasing power, velocity as turnover risk, and interest rates
      as credibility instruments.

  (2) Documents how the software computes currency index and health metrics.

  (3) Evaluates preset scenarios as stress cases analogous to crisis and spiral regimes.

  (4) Discusses portfolio and institutional implications within the model’s limits.

1.3 Relation to the Economics Thesis

A companion economics thesis emphasises quantity theory, fiscal–monetary interaction,
and pedagogy. The present document emphasises asset erosion, risk metrics, and
financial stability intuition. Both refer to the same codebase (simulationStore.ts).

2. FINANCIAL ECONOMICS OF HIGH INFLATION
================================================================================

2.1 Currency as an Asset

Holding domestic currency is equivalent to holding a nominal claim with zero coupon
and uncertain real return. Expected real return ≈ nominal interest minus expected
inflation minus risk premium. When inflation exceeds nominal rates, real returns on
cash are deeply negative—classic financial repression. Agents reduce balances (high V)
and shift to goods, foreign currency, or indexed assets.

2.2 Fisher Equation and Credibility

The Fisher relation links nominal rates, real rates, and inflation expectations. In
the simulator, the policy rate i enters twice: it dampens real output growth (tight
money channel) and supports confidence (credibility channel). Low i during high
printing mimics negative real policy rates—a hallmark of monetised fiscal stress.

2.3 Velocity and Liquidity Risk

Velocity rises when money is a “hot potato.” Financially, this is liquidity risk on
the monetary base: holders minimise exposure duration. The model sets V = 1 when
confidence is maximal and up to roughly 3.2 when confidence is minimal, feeding back
into inflation through Φ.

2.4 Sovereign Finance Sketch

Although the simulator does not model bond markets explicitly, the sliders map to
familiar sovereign trade-offs:

  • High G with low τ → implicit need for monetisation or debt (printing slider).
  • High π_m → seigniorage / deficit financing.
  • Low i → accommodative stance, weak currency support.
  • High i → stabilisation but growth drag.

The “spiral” preset is a caricature of unsustainable sovereign finance: large spending,
low tax effort, aggressive monetisation, near-zero rates.

2.5 Literature Touchpoints

  • Cagan money-demand instability during hyperinflation.
  • Bruno and Fischer (1990) on stabilisation and budget constraints.
  • Reinhart and Rogoff (2009) on default and inflation history.
  • Modern EM sovereign analysis (Eichengreen, Hausmann) on original sin and
    dollarisation—conceptually relevant though not modelled here.

3. FRAMEWORK: BALANCE SHEETS AND FLOWS IN REDUCED FORM
================================================================================

3.1 Simplified Household–Government Split

Represent the private sector as holding real money balances M/P (proxied by index M
with fixed baseline price level normalisation). Government spending G boosts output
index Y; taxes τ withdraw resources. Central bank accommodation appears as π_m.

3.2 Nominal vs Real Wealth

  • Money supply M grows at rate π_m each month.
  • Purchasing power index κ erodes when π > 0: κ' = κ · 100/(100 + π).
  • After n months of constant π, κ ≈ κ₀ · (100/(100+π))^n — geometric decay of
    real value.

This is the key financial series for “how much does a unit of currency buy.”

3.3 Confidence as Intangible Capital

Confidence C acts like institutional capital affecting velocity and inflation premia.
Loss of C is analogous to rising country risk premium: faster pass-through of money
growth to prices and harder stabilisation.

================================================================================
4. MODEL FROM A FINANCE LENS
================================================================================

4.1 Inputs and State (Monthly)

  Controls: π_m (printing), G (spending index), i (rate), τ (tax)
  States: M, Y, C, V (derived), κ, π (current inflation)

4.2 Return on Holding Currency

Implicit monthly real loss on cash when inflation is π and no interest on M:

  real_erosion ≈ −π/(100+π)  [from κ update, first-order]

Nominal deposits paying i are not modelled separately; i affects inflation only through
policy channels above.

4.3 Inflation as Risk Factor

  π = max(−2, π_m − g_Y + Φ)

where g_Y is clamped real growth and

  Φ = (1 − C/100)·3.2 + max(0, V−1)·2.4

Φ is a risk premium-like shifter: when confidence is low, inflation exceeds naive
money-growth-minus-output arithmetic.

4.4 Feedback Loop (Financial Stability View)

  High π → lower C → higher V and Φ → higher π → lower κ

This positive feedback is the financial stability concern: once κ and C deteriorate,
voluntary dollarisation or indexation in real economies becomes rational; the model
captures the macro precursor without modelling contract denomination.

4.5 Interest Rate as Stabilisation Tool

Each point of i adds 0.12 to C per month and subtracts from g_Y. Finance interpretation:
the central bank trades short-term output cost for credibility, reducing future Φ.
Whether this stabilises κ depends on whether inflation falls faster than growth damage.

================================================================================
5. METRICS: CURRENCY INDEX AND HEALTH
================================================================================

5.1 Currency Index κ

  • Baseline 100 at reset or preset load.
  • Not an FX rate against USD; domestic purchasing power only.
  • Declines multiplicatively with positive monthly inflation.

Use case: illustrate cumulative wealth erosion on nominal cash positions.

5.2 Currency Health (0–100)

  health = clamp(0.85·C − 1.8·min(55, max(0,π)) + 12, 0, 100)

Interpretation:

  • Base weight on confidence (85% scale).
  • Inflation penalty up to 55 points (capped).
  • Offset +12 for UI baseline.

Health is a composite risk dashboard metric, not a market price. It correlates with
warning bands: stable (π<5%), unstable (5–25%), hyperinflation (π≥25%).

5.3 Annualised Inflation Display

UI shows 12×π (simple). For finance users, compounded annual inflation would be
(1+π/100)^12 − 1; the simulator uses the simpler display deliberately.

5.4 Charted Series

  • Inflation % (left axis)
  • Money supply M (right axis)
  • Currency index κ (right axis)

Joint movement: in spiral preset, M rises while κ falls—classic “more money, less
value per unit.”

6. SCENARIO AND RISK ANALYSIS
================================================================================

6.1 Stable Preset — Baseline Risk

  π_m=0.9%, G=42, i=5%, τ=24%, C₀=75

Financial reading: moderate monetisation, credible rate, adequate tax effort. κ
typically declines slowly if at all; health remains elevated. Suitable benchmark for
“investable” domestic currency regime in stylised terms.

6.2 Crisis Preset — Elevated Sovereign Stress

  π_m=4.5%, G=82, i=1.5%, τ=14%, C₀=55

Financial reading: fiscal expansion with weak monetisation restraint and low rates.
Negative real policy stance. Expect κ to fall sharply within quarters (simulated
months); velocity rises as C falls. Unstable warning band likely.

6.3 Spiral Preset — Currency Collapse Regime

  π_m=14%, G=92, i=0.5%, τ=8%, C₀=38

Financial reading: extreme monetisation (14% M growth per month), maximal spending
pressure, minimal tax, near-zero rates, low initial trust. Hyperinflation warnings
(π≥25%) emerge quickly; κ approaches small fractions of 100 within a year of simulated
time. Equivalent to holding cash with −14% nominal expansion plus inflation premium Φ.

6.4 Sensitivity Themes for Finance Students

  A. Printing dominates when Φ is large (low C).
  B. Rate hikes support C but may reduce g_Y—stabilisation trilemma in miniature.
  C. Fiscal expansion without tax backing raises g_Y short-term but fuels π via
     sustained monetisation if π_m is high.
  D. Path dependence: early rate hike may prevent spiral; late hike may be insufficient.

6.5 Risk Metrics Not in Model

  • FX forward premia, CDS spreads, capital controls
  • Term structure of interest rates
  • Bank balance-sheet dollarisation
  • Crypto or informal dollarisation

These are discussed as real-world extensions.

7. IMPLICATIONS FOR PORTFOLIO AND INSTITUTIONAL FINANCE
================================================================================

7.1 Nominal Cash and Deposits

Under spiral dynamics, nominal cash loses purchasing power exponentially in κ. Real
assets (commodities, foreign currency, indexed bonds) dominate in theory; the
simulator motivates why EM portfolios overweight hard currency and inflation hedges
during stress.

7.2 Equities and Real Output

Y may still grow under high G, but π erodes valuation multiples when discount rates
rise with inflation risk. The model does not price equities; the lesson is that
nominal GDP growth from inflation does not imply real wealth preservation.

7.3 Fixed Income

Nominal bonds are devastated in hyperinflation; inflation-linked instruments require
credible indexation institutions absent here. Rate i in the model is a policy lever,
not a market yield curve.

7.4 Central Bank and Banking Sector

High V implies rapid deposit turnover and potential disintermediation. Banks cannot
profit from maturity transformation when real rates are deeply negative. Financial
repression literature applies.

7.5 Policy Game for Stabilisation

Finance-oriented stabilisation typically requires:

  • Fiscal consolidation (higher τ or lower G in the model)
  • Monetary restraint (lower π_m, higher i)
  • Confidence restoration (outcome of the above)

The simulator lets users explore sequencing—e.g., rate hike without fiscal adjustment
may fail if π_m remains high.

8. IMPLEMENTATION NOTES
================================================================================

8.1 Technology

React + TypeScript + Zustand + Recharts; client-only, no persistence. computeStep()
in simulationStore.ts is the single source of financial–macro dynamics.

8.2 Control Flow

SimulationControls.tsx runs tick() every 1000 ms when running. PolicyPanel sliders
update store; changes affect next month.

8.3 Reproducibility

All coefficients are literals in source code—suitable for assignment questions (“raise
credibility elasticity from 3.2 to 4.0 and compare κ paths”).

================================================================================
9. LIMITATIONS AND EXTENSIONS
================================================================================

9.1 Limitations

  • No FX market, no carry trades, no capital account.
  • No explicit government debt or default.
  • No asset prices beyond κ proxy.
  • Linear/threshold rules rather than estimated finance models.

9.2 Possible Extensions

  • Add foreign-currency asset with exchange rate tied to κ.
  • Model indexed vs nominal bonds with different returns.
  • Introduce inflation expectations as forward-looking state.
  • Calibrate presets to historical episodes (Zimbabwe, etc.) with disclaimers.

================================================================================
10. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

The Hyperinflation Control Simulator offers finance students and practitioners a
transparent environment to internalise how monetisation, fiscal expansion, and
credibility interact to destroy purchasing power. The currency index κ and health
metric translate macro policy into wealth erosion and stability risk. Presets span
from stabilisation-friendly parameters to spiral regimes that replicate financial
chaos in reduced form.

Used with clear boundaries, the tool bridges monetary economics and financial risk
management. It does not replace market data or sovereign models, but it clarifies
why, in extreme regimes, the first priority is to stop the loss of real value in the
domestic currency—and why that requires coordinated fiscal and monetary credibility.
