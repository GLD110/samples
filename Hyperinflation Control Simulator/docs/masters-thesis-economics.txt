ABSTRACT
================================================================================

Hyperinflation episodes combine rapid monetary expansion, collapsing institutional
credibility, and behavioural acceleration of money circulation. This thesis develops
and documents the Hyperinflation Control Simulator, a client-side computational
laboratory that translates classical quantity-theoretic intuition and modern
macro policy trade-offs into an interactive monthly time-step model.

The simulator implements four policy levers—money printing, government spending,
the policy interest rate, and the tax rate—alongside endogenous state variables
for money supply, real output, public confidence, and the velocity of money. Monthly
inflation is specified as money growth minus real output growth plus terms that
capture confidence erosion and velocity feedback. Confidence itself responds to
inflation surprises and interest-rate credibility. The model is deliberately
reduced-form: it prioritises transparent feedback loops over empirical calibration.

Three preset scenarios (stable economy, crisis economy, hyperinflation spiral)
illustrate how parameter constellations map to qualitatively different dynamic paths.
The thesis argues that such tools complement formal theory by making fiscal–monetary
interaction and positive feedback visible to students and policymakers, while
clearly delineating limits: the simulator is not a forecasting engine and does not
replace econometric identification.

Keywords: hyperinflation, quantity theory, fiscal policy, monetary policy, simulation,
credibility, velocity of money, interactive macroeconomics.

1. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

1.1 Motivation

Episodes of very high inflation—commonly labelled hyperinflation when monthly rates
exceed roughly 50 percent in the academic convention, though operational definitions
vary—remain among the most destructive macroeconomic crises. They destroy real
balances, distort relative prices, undermine tax collection, and often coincide with
political instability. Yet the mechanisms that generate and sustain hyperinflation
are teachable: excessive monetisation of fiscal deficits, loss of confidence in the
currency unit, and strategic behaviour that accelerates spending before money loses
value.

Traditional instruction relies on static diagrams (AD–AS shifts, Phillips curves) or
historical case studies (Weimar Germany, Zimbabwe, Venezuela). Both are indispensable
but leave the temporal structure of feedback implicit. A discrete-time simulation
allows learners to manipulate levers and observe compounding month by month.

1.2 Research Question

This thesis asks: Can a transparent, reduced-form monthly macro model, implemented
as an interactive web application, faithfully convey the core economic mechanisms
of inflation control and hyperinflation risk without claiming empirical precision?

1.3 Contribution

The contribution is threefold:

  (a) A documented specification linking policy sliders to inflation, output, and
      confidence dynamics, grounded in quantity-theoretic decomposition.

  (b) An open implementation (React, TypeScript, Zustand) that exposes all
      coefficients for classroom experimentation.

  (c) A critical assessment of what such pedagogy can and cannot establish about
      real economies.

1.4 Scope

The analysis is positive–pedagogical, not econometric. No country data are loaded;
coefficients are chosen for qualitative plausibility and visual clarity.

2. LITERATURE AND CONCEPTUAL BACKGROUND
================================================================================

2.1 The Quantity Theory and Its Limits

The identity MV = PY, with M money stock, V velocity, P price level, and Y real
output, underpins much inflation discourse. In growth-rate form, approximate
inflation equals money growth plus velocity growth minus output growth, when
definitions are held consistent. Friedman’s dictum that inflation is “always and
everywhere a monetary phenomenon” emphasises sustained excess money creation;
sceptics note reverse causation, financial innovation, and open-economy channels.

The simulator adopts the growth-rate decomposition as its organising principle but
augments it with a confidence–velocity block absent from naive textbook versions.

2.2 Fiscal Theory and Monetisation

When governments spend beyond politically feasible taxation and credible debt
issuance, central banks may accommodate deficits through base money creation. The
fiscal theory of the price level (FTPL) and classic “unpleasant monetarist
arithmetic” (Sargent and Wallace, 1981) stress that unsustainable fiscal paths can
force inflation even if monetary authorities attempt restraint in the short run.
Government spending in the simulator raises real output growth directly—a reduced-form
stand-in for demand stimulus—while high money printing expands nominal balances.

2.3 Credibility and Expectations

Kydland and Prescott’s time-inconsistency problem and Barro–Gordon inflation bias
highlight that announced disinflation fails without credible commitment. In crisis
episodes, “confidence” collapses: households and firms shorten money-holding periods,
raising V. The simulator’s confidence variable (0–99 scale) formalises this as a
state that both enters the inflation equation and is eroded by high realised inflation,
while interest rates partially restore it.

2.4 Hyperinflation Case Studies

Cagan (1956) modelled money demand during hyperinflations as sensitive to expected
inflation, generating unstable dynamics when monetisation persists. Hanke and
Krus (2012) catalogue historical episodes, emphasising fiscal roots. These histories
inform the “spiral” preset: aggressive printing, high spending, low rates, low initial
confidence.

2.5 Computational Economics and Teaching

Agent-based models and DSGE simulators serve researchers; lightweight browser tools
serve classrooms. The present project occupies the latter niche, comparable in spirit
to classroom Phillips-curve simulators but focused on high-inflation regimes.

3. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
================================================================================

3.1 Structure

The economy is represented at monthly frequency with:

  Policy inputs (chosen by user):
    π_m   money printing rate (% per month)
    G     government spending index (15–100)
    i     policy interest rate (%)
    τ     tax rate (%)

  State variables:
    M     money supply index (baseline 100)
    Y     real output index (baseline 100)
    C     confidence index (8–99)
    V     velocity (derived)
    κ     currency purchasing-power index (baseline 100)

  Outcome:
    π     monthly inflation rate (%)

3.2 Real Output Growth Channel

Real output growth combines a small autonomous trend with fiscal stimulus and
drag from taxation and high interest rates:

  g_Y = 0.2 + 0.55·(G/100) − 0.5·(τ/100) − 0.35·(i/100)    [% per month]

Then g_Y is clamped to [−1.2, 4] to prevent explosive or deeply negative growth
from slider extremes. This block captures the idea that spending supports activity
while taxes and tight money cool it—without modelling a full IS curve or government
budget constraint explicitly.

3.3 Velocity and the “Hot Potato”

When confidence is low, agents reduce desired real money balances:

  V = 1 + ((100 − C) / 100) · 2.4

At C = 100, V = 1; at C = 8, V approaches 3.2. Higher velocity, holding M fixed,
raises nominal spending pressure—analogous to faster turnover of a depreciating
asset.

3.4 Inflation Equation

Define a confidence–velocity factor:

  Φ = (1 − C/100) · 3.2 + max(0, V − 1) · 2.4

Monthly inflation is:

  π = max(−2,  π_m − g_Y + Φ)    [% per month]

Interpretation: direct money growth (π_m) pushes prices up; real output growth
(g_Y) absorbs some nominal expansion; low confidence and high velocity add
inflationary pressure beyond simple monetarist arithmetic. The floor at −2% allows
mild deflationary months under tight policy but prevents unrealistic large negative
inflation from dominating the UI.

3.5 Confidence Dynamics

Confidence updates each month:

  • Erosion when π > 1.8%: subtract 0.55 · max(0, π − 1.8)
  • Credibility gain from interest rates: add 0.12 · i
  • Recovery when π < 2.5%: add 0.35 + 0.04 · (68 − C) toward a neutral band
  • Clamp C to [8, 99]

This creates a two-way feedback: high inflation lowers confidence, which raises Φ
and thus future inflation—mimicking spiral risk without solving rational expectations
explicitly.

3.6 Stock Accumulation

  M_{t+1} = M_t · (1 + π_m/100)
  Y_{t+1} = Y_t · (1 + g_Y/100)
  κ_{t+1} = κ_t · 100/(100 + max(0, π))    [purchasing power erosion]

The currency index κ is not an exchange rate; it tracks domestic purchasing power
relative to baseline 100.

================================================================================
4. MODEL SPECIFICATION
================================================================================

4.1 Warning Thresholds

The user interface classifies monthly inflation as:

  Stable:          π < 5%
  Unstable:        5% ≤ π < 25%
  Hyperinflation:  π ≥ 25%

These thresholds are pedagogical markers aligned with the simulator’s scale (monthly
percent), not a claim about international statistical definitions.

4.2 Currency Health Metric

A composite “currency health” meter (0–100) combines confidence and inflation:

  health = clamp(0.85·C − 1.8·min(55, max(0, π)) + 12,  0, 100)

High inflation penalises health even if confidence has not yet fully collapsed.

4.3 Annualisation Display

The UI shows “annualized est.” as π × 12 (simple multiplication, not compounding).
This is a rough heuristic for readers accustomed to annual CPI, not a rigorous
annual rate.

4.4 Time Discretisation

One real-time second equals one simulated month while the simulation runs. History
retains up to 72 months. Users may pause, reset, or load presets without server
persistence.

================================================================================
5. IMPLEMENTATION AND USER INTERFACE
================================================================================

5.1 Software Architecture

The application is built with React and TypeScript, bundled by Vite. Global state
resides in a Zustand store (simulationStore.ts). The central function computeStep()
encapsulates all economic updating; tick() advances the clock and appends chart
points. Recharts renders inflation, money supply, and currency index series.

5.2 Policy Panel

Sliders bound approximately:

  Money printing:     0–25% / month
  Government spending: 15–100
  Interest rate:      0–18%
  Tax rate:           5–45%

Changes apply on the next tick after pause or during continuous run.

5.3 Separation of Concerns

By isolating economics in computeStep(), instructors may alter coefficients or add
equations without restructuring UI components—a maintainability choice that supports
thesis replication exercises.

6. SCENARIO ANALYSIS
================================================================================

6.1 Default Initial State

  π_m = 1.2%, G = 48, i = 4.5%, τ = 22%
  M = Y = κ = 100, C = 72

Under default settings, moderate printing and mid-range fiscal policy typically
produce inflation in the unstable band if confidence erodes, or stabilise if rates
support credibility—outcomes depend on path and duration.

6.2 Preset: Stable Economy

  π_m = 0.9%, G = 42, i = 5%, τ = 24%, C₀ = 75

Lower monetisation, balanced spending, higher taxes, and stronger initial confidence
favour monthly inflation below 5% and gradual recovery of confidence when shocks
are absent.

6.3 Preset: Crisis Economy

  π_m = 4.5%, G = 82, i = 1.5%, τ = 14%, C₀ = 55

Loose money, high spending, low rates, and weak confidence push π into unstable or
hyperinflation bands within several months. Velocity rises as confidence falls,
amplifying Φ.

6.4 Preset: Hyperinflation Spiral

  π_m = 14%, G = 92, i = 0.5%, τ = 8%, C₀ = 38

This constellation models aggressive deficit monetisation and fiscal expansion with
minimal monetary restraint. Money supply grows at 14% per month; real growth may be
positive from spending but cannot offset printing and confidence effects. Currency
index κ collapses rapidly; warnings persist in hyperinflation state.

6.5 Policy Experiments (Qualitative)

Students can test:

  (1) Raising i to stabilise C and reduce Φ—at the cost of lower g_Y.
  (2) Cutting π_m with lagged effect on M growth.
  (3) Fiscal tightening via higher τ or lower G to cool demand.
  (4) Interaction effects: low rates during high printing accelerate spirals.

Formal comparative statics are limited because the system is nonlinear and path-dependent.

================================================================================
7. PEDAGOGICAL VALUE AND LIMITATIONS
================================================================================

7.1 Strengths

  • Makes MV = PY logic operational month by month.
  • Shows positive feedback between inflation and confidence.
  • Links fiscal and monetary instruments in one dashboard.
  • Immediate visual feedback lowers barrier for non-specialists.

7.2 Limitations

  • No microfoundations, optimisation, or expectations equilibrium.
  • No open economy, exchange rates, or dollarisation.
  • No government budget constraint or debt stock.
  • Coefficients are not estimated; calibration is illustrative.
  • Monthly hyperinflation threshold (25%) is a UI convention, not Cagan’s 50%.

7.3 Relation to Empirical Work

Forecasting real CPI requires data, identification, and structural or time-series
models. This simulator should never be cited as evidence for a specific country’s
trajectory.

================================================================================
8. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

The Hyperinflation Control Simulator distils decades of monetary and fiscal theory
into a compact feedback system suitable for interactive learning. Its inflation
equation embodies money growth minus real growth plus confidence-driven velocity
pressure; its confidence dynamics capture credibility loss and partial restoration
through interest rates.

For economics master’s training, the project demonstrates how to translate theory
into reproducible code, how to communicate limits honestly, and how simulation can
complement—not replace—rigorous empirical macroeconomics. Future extensions might
add explicit deficit financing, seigniorage, wage indexation, or heterogeneous
expectations; each would move the model toward research-grade complexity at the
cost of transparency.
